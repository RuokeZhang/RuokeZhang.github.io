failure

During a big promotion our model’s TPM (500,000 tokens/min) was exhausted and many highlight requests started failing. My goal was to stabilize the service quickly without just dropping requests. I added a worker-side token bucket (Redis) so a task only calls the model if tokens are available, otherwise it gets delayed and retried later. I also tied autoscaling to token usage and queue length so we automatically add capacity when pressure rises. Within minutes the failure rate fell below 1% and the task queue stopped growing.

interpersonal conflict

On one project my teammate thought the prompt-engineering tasks were too simple and didn’t want to join. He asked me to do everything, but it was too much for one person because we needed to evaluate multiple model APIs on time. I set up a one-on-one chat to hear his concerns.

I said I understood the tasks felt basic, but I pointed out that delivering on time matters for the team’s reputation. I suggested we split the work: I’d take the repetitive parts while he focused on module design and strategic decisions. I also explained that finishing this well would lead to more challenging opportunities. He agreed, we delivered on schedule, and our work quality led to tougher assignments and a better working relationship.

new task

I was asked to build a quota management API and integrate it with DMP and real-time PV tracking. I wasn’t familiar with the ad business—especially A/B traffic setup and DMP—so I invested time understanding those first. I confirmed acceptance criteria with the PM, then split the work into core components: the quota query API, the allocation algorithm using urgency-based greedy approach, real-time PV tracking integration, and VID polling for A/B experiment traffic ratios.

For each component, I read docs, studied existing code, and talked to service owners. I also built a quick prototype to validate assumptions and added metrics and end-to-end tests. We delivered on time; the system raised campaign CTR by 3%, supports 5M daily users, and the prototype became the foundation for automation.

why expedia

I use Expedia frequently and I appreciate the scale it has achieved. I can book hotels almost anywhere—even in remote parts of South America—and I can pay in the currency I prefer. I’ve also rented cars through Expedia and had a great experience.

Expedia provides a full travel ecosystem—flights, hotels, cars, and packages—which means engineers there build products that affect millions of travelers’ real lives. I’m proud of the idea of building software that people rely on when they travel.

As an SDE intern, I want to work on backend and systems challenges: scaling services, improving reliability, and streamlining booking flows so small improvements translate to better experiences for users worldwide. My experience building high-throughput pipelines at TikTok gives me the technical background to contribute quickly and learn on the job.

During my internship at TikTok, there was a department meeting where another team mentioned they needed to integrate the Indonesian seller FAQ into the RAG-based chatbot, but they were short on engineers. My team was focused on a critical deadline, so they couldn’t support the work.

After the meeting, I approached my mentor and volunteered to own this task. He connected me with that team, and I began driving the integration. I read their design docs, learned how the RAG pipeline worked, and worked closely with the operations team to make sure I understood their goals.

After about one week, the Indonesian seller FAQ was fully integrated into the chatbot. Internal tests showed about 95% answer accuracy, and the service went online ahead of schedule with weekly content updates.
